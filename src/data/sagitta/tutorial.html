title: Drawing the snake lemma standfirst: A quick tutorial by example. description: The snake lemma in Sagitta, key by key: rows of objects, rectangle select, checkpoints, and an arrow that crosses over.

I quite like the Wikipedia snake lemma figure. Let’s recreate it in Sagitta.

We’ll start by making each row of the diagram. SPC on an empty cell creates a vertex. If a vertex is already selected, SPC also draws an arrow from it to the new vertex. Pressing SPC and → alternately a few times gives a sequence of bullets. Let’s make four:

[image seq.png | Four bullets in a row. | A Sagitta screenshot of four bullets in a row, each mapping right to the next.]

We could either fill in their labels now or wait until later. One way is to hit TAB repeatedly, which cycles through the unlabelled vertices and arrows so that we can edit their labels. If we didn’t use TAB cycling, we could select arrows for labelling by / plus whatever code the arrow got assigned (usually, the letters of its endpoints concatenated). Here, the arrow we need to label \(g\) got assigned SD.

[image search.png | Selecting an arrow by its code. | A Sagitta screenshot of search: the row A by f to B to C to 0, each vertex and arrow tagged with its code (A, S, D, and F for the vertices; AS, SD, and DF for the arrows).]

Next, ESC clears the selection, and we build the rest of the objects in rows. When it’s time to connect the rows together, we clear our selection, press SPC on the source vertex, and then SPC on the target. If we loaded a custom macros.tex file, we could’ve defined \coker as \operatorname{coker}, but for this quick example, we’ll write it directly. With most everything but the connecting morphism done, we have:

[image grid.png | Both rows, joined, with the kernels and cokernels in sequence. | A Sagitta screenshot of a commutative diagram with two exact rows, A by f to B to C by g to 0, and 0 to A′ by f′ to B′ by g′ to C′. Vertical maps connect them: A by a to A′, B by b to B′, and C by c to C′. Each vertical map sits in a sequence: ker a to A to A′ to coker a, ker b to B to B′ to coker b, and ker c to C to C′ to coker c.]

Because we want our connecting morphism \(d\) to cut right through the middle of the diagram, we should move half the diagram down to clear some space. We’ll select the seven lower vertices with rectangle select: C-SPC on one corner and C-SPC on the opposite corner collect everything in between:

[image select_bottom.png | Rectangle select, from corner to corner. | A Sagitta screenshot of the same diagram, with the second row and the cokernels selected.]

We could also use / with a space-separated list, or even hold Shift and move our cursor over each cell. Once everything is selected, C-↓ moves the whole selection down:

[image move_bottom.png | The lower half moved down, clearing a lane through the centre. | A Sagitta screenshot of the same diagram, with the second row and the cokernels moved down to make room.]

Now we’ll make the trickiest morphism of the diagram! Creating an arrow the usual way between \(\ker c\) and \(\operatorname{coker} a\) isn’t enough, since it’ll cut across the diagram centre inelegantly. We create the arrow, select it, and move the cursor two cells right of \(\ker c\). Hitting k adds that cell to the arrow’s route as a checkpoint (pressing k again would remove it).

[image start_curve.png | The first checkpoint, bending the arrow out of the way. | A Sagitta screenshot of the same diagram, with an arrow from ker c to coker a partway around it.]

We add the rest of the checkpoints the same way.

[image curve_done.png | The connecting morphism, routed the long way round. | A Sagitta screenshot of the same diagram, with the arrow from ker c to coker a routed all the way around through its checkpoints.]

In the original Wikipedia image, the connecting morphism lies over the arrows in the middle. With the connecting morphism still selected, we can just hit x to let it cross over:

[image curve_cut.png | Crossing over, cutting a gap in the arrows beneath. | A Sagitta screenshot of the same diagram, with the connecting morphism crossing over the arrows a, b, and c in the centre and hiding their labels.]

Nearly there, but the labels are all wonky: \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are hidden underneath the connecting morphism! We select each arrow in turn, hit P, and shift the label position along the arrow using the arrow keys. Let’s do the same for the connecting morphism, and make sure the label renders along the outside of the arrow by hitting A for label alignment and then r for right. Finally, we hide the grid with # and enjoy!

[image complete.png | The finished diagram. | A Sagitta screenshot of the finished diagram, with two exact rows, A by f to B to C by g to 0, and 0 to A′ by f′ to B′ by g′ to C′. Vertical maps connect them: A by a to A′, B by b to B′, and C by c to C′. Each vertical map sits in a sequence: ker a to A to A′ to coker a, ker b to B to B′ to coker b, and ker c to C to C′ to coker c. A connecting morphism d snakes from ker c to coker a, crossing over the centre of the diagram.]

I’ll just save it to a file with C-s and put it in my very cool paper on the snake lemma.
